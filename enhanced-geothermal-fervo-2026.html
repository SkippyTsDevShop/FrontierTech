Enhanced Geothermal Just Achieved First Power in Utah

Fervo Energy borrowed horizontal drilling from the oil and gas industry to unlock geothermal energy almost anywhere — not just Iceland-style hot spots. Its Cape Station project just hit a milestone no utility-scale enhanced geothermal plant had reached before.

By the FrontierTech Desk·September 27, 2026·7 min read

Illustration: FrontierTech.news

On September 24, 2026, Fervo Energy's Cape Station project in Beaver County, Utah, achieved what the company calls "first power" — the first time a utility-scale enhanced geothermal system (EGS) has actually started delivering electricity anywhere in the world. It's a meaningful milestone for a technology that spent decades as a promising idea that never quite left the pilot-project stage.

Why geothermal needed "enhancing" in the first place

Conventional geothermal power only works in a narrow set of places: locations like Iceland or parts of Nevada where naturally hot, permeable, water-saturated rock sits close enough to the surface to tap directly. That's a small fraction of the planet. Enhanced geothermal systems get around that by engineering the missing piece — drilling into hot rock that isn't naturally permeable, then creating the fracture network and fluid circulation that a natural hydrothermal site would otherwise provide.

What Fervo actually changed

Fervo's specific contribution is borrowing horizontal drilling and multi-well-pad techniques directly from the shale oil and gas industry — the same technology that made fracking commercially viable — and applying it to geothermal wells instead. That lets the company drill multiple horizontal wells from a single surface pad into deep, hot rock that was previously inaccessible with older vertical-drilling geothermal methods, and Fervo says its per-well drilling rate has improved 143% since its first Cape Station well, evidence the technique is getting cheaper and faster with practice rather than staying a one-off engineering feat.

The scale, in context

Cape Station's first phase is roughly 100 megawatts across three 33-megawatt units, with a second phase adding 400 megawatts and roughly 900 megawatts contracted overall once fully built out. That's a meaningful, if not massive, power plant — comparable to a mid-sized natural gas peaker plant — but the significance is less about this one project's size and more about proving the drilling economics work at utility scale, which is the step that determines whether EGS becomes a real category or stays a niche.

Why data centers specifically care

Unlike solar and wind, geothermal runs around the clock regardless of weather, which makes it a closer substitute for the steady, always-on power that data centers actually need — the same underlying demand driving the current nuclear and small-modular-reactor buildout we've covered elsewhere. That's exactly why Fervo has attracted attention from hyperscale data-center operators looking for firm, carbon-free power that isn't nuclear and doesn't depend on a specific weather pattern.

The honest caveats

First power is not full capacity, and Cape Station's full 900-megawatt build-out is still years away. Enhanced geothermal also remains meaningfully more expensive per megawatt than conventional geothermal or utility-scale solar, and Fervo's drilling-cost improvements, however real, have so far been demonstrated at one company's one project rather than validated as an industry-wide cost curve. Whether EGS becomes a genuine grid-scale category or stays a well-funded niche depends on whether Fervo's economics hold up as it scales past Cape Station — and on whether competitors can replicate the approach.

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes Fervo Energy's geothermal technology 'enhanced'?

Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) engineer permeability into hot rock that wouldn't naturally support geothermal power, using horizontal drilling and multi-well pads borrowed from the shale oil and gas industry, rather than relying on naturally occurring hydrothermal sites like Iceland's.

How big is the Cape Station project?

Phase one is about 100 megawatts across three 33-megawatt units, with a second phase adding 400 megawatts and roughly 900 megawatts contracted overall once the project is fully built out.

Why do data centers care about geothermal specifically?

Geothermal runs continuously regardless of weather, providing the same kind of firm, around-the-clock power that data centers need and that intermittent sources like solar and wind can't guarantee on their own.

Is enhanced geothermal cost-competitive yet?

Not fully. It remains more expensive per megawatt than conventional geothermal or utility-scale solar today, though Fervo says its drilling costs have fallen sharply with each successive well, and the real test is whether that cost curve holds as the company scales beyond its first project.
