The Ultimate Guide to Stablecoins

Stablecoins now settle more value monthly than the US ACH network. Here's what they actually are, who dominates the market, and how the 2025 GENIUS Act changed the rules.

By the FrontierTech Desk·September 28, 2026·8 min read

Illustration: FrontierTech.news

A stablecoin is a cryptocurrency designed to hold a stable value, almost always pegged one-to-one to the US dollar, by backing each token with reserves — cash, short-term Treasuries, or other liquid assets — held by the issuer. The pitch is simple: get the speed and programmability of a crypto asset without the price swings that make Bitcoin or Ether impractical as everyday money. In 2026, that pitch has scaled into a genuinely large piece of financial infrastructure.

How big the market actually is

The stablecoin market reached roughly $308 billion in total capitalization as of August 2026, up about 14.3 percent from the year before. Two issuers dominate: Tether's USDT holds around 59 percent of total supply, and Circle's USDC holds about 23 percent — together commanding roughly 82 percent of the market. The rest is split among smaller players including Sky Dollar, DAI, and newer entrants.

Supply share and actual usage tell different stories, though. USDC led 2025's annual transaction volume at $18.3 trillion versus USDT's $13.3 trillion, while USDT still dominates centralized exchange trading at roughly 74 percent of that volume — meaning USDT is used more for trading, and USDC circulates more broadly through payments and settlement.

What stablecoins are actually used for

The headline transaction numbers are misleading if read as real-economy spending. Gross stablecoin transfers ran somewhere between $28 and $62 trillion in 2025, but actual real-economy payments — merchants, payroll, remittances — represent only an estimated $350 to $550 billion of that, or roughly 1 percent. The overwhelming majority of stablecoin volume is trading, arbitrage, and moving value between exchanges and DeFi protocols, not paying for goods and services.

Stablecoin settlements reached $7.2 trillion in a single month in early 2026, surpassing the entire US ACH network's $6.8 trillion for the first time — a genuine infrastructure milestone, even if most of that volume is still crypto-native activity rather than retail payments.

The GENIUS Act changed the rules mid-2026

Stablecoins operated in a regulatory gray zone in the US until the GENIUS Act became law in 2025, establishing the first comprehensive federal framework and creating a formal category called "permitted payment stablecoin issuers." Throughout 2026, Treasury, the OCC, and the FDIC have been writing the actual operational rules issuers have to follow — and the emerging picture is less about reserve requirements, which the law itself already set, and more about anti-money-laundering compliance, treating stablecoin issuers similarly to how banks are regulated for that purpose.

Why institutions preferred a rulebook, even a strict one

A demanding compliance framework is generally viewed by banks and asset managers as preferable to regulatory ambiguity, because ambiguity is what kept large institutional players on the sidelines for years. That shift is visible in the growth of tokenized Treasury products, which use stablecoin-adjacent infrastructure to let institutional money move in and out of yield-bearing assets on-chain — a market that only became viable at scale once issuers had a real legal category to operate inside of.

What's still unresolved

The operational rules under the GENIUS Act remain open for public comment as of September 2026, meaning the specific compliance requirements — and their cost to issuers — could still change before finalization. Smaller stablecoin issuers may struggle to absorb the compliance costs a bank-style AML program requires, which could accelerate consolidation toward the handful of issuers, like Tether and Circle, that already dominate supply. Whether that concentration is a feature (fewer points of failure) or a risk (less competition, more systemic exposure to two companies) is the debate regulators and the market are still having.

Frequently Asked Questions

What backs a stablecoin's value?

Reserves held by the issuer — typically cash and short-term US Treasuries — equal to the number of tokens in circulation, so each token can theoretically be redeemed for one dollar.

Which stablecoin is the biggest?

USDT (Tether) holds roughly 59% of total stablecoin supply, followed by USDC (Circle) at about 23%. Together they represent about 82% of the $308 billion market as of August 2026.

Are stablecoins mostly used for everyday payments?

No. Estimates suggest real-economy payments make up only about 1% of gross stablecoin transfer volume; the large majority is trading, arbitrage, and movement between exchanges and DeFi protocols.

What did the GENIUS Act actually change?

It created the first comprehensive federal framework for stablecoins, including a formal issuer category with reserve and disclosure requirements. Throughout 2026, regulators have been writing the operational rules, which are turning out to focus heavily on anti-money-laundering compliance.
