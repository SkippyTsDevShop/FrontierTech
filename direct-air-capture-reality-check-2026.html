The World's Largest Carbon-Capture Plant Just Hit a Snag

Occidental's Stratos plant was supposed to prove direct air capture could work at real scale. In 2026, an unrelated equipment problem pushed back its launch again — the latest sign that DAC's hardest problem might be execution, not chemistry.

By the FrontierTech Desk·September 27, 2026·6 min read

Illustration: FrontierTech.news

Direct air capture has one job: pull carbon dioxide straight out of ambient air and bury it underground, permanently. It's the most literal version of carbon removal there is, and Occidental's Stratos facility in Ector County, Texas, was built to prove it could work at a genuinely industrial scale — 500,000 metric tons of CO2 removed per year, the largest DAC plant in the world. In 2026, an equipment problem pushed its launch back for the third time.

What actually went wrong

According to Occidental's own quarterly earnings call, the delay isn't a chemistry or technology failure. Chief Operating Officer Richard Jackson described it as "an issue related to non-process components of the facility, which are not related to the technology" — discovered after the plant's first unit had already completed initial commissioning tests and performed as expected. In plain terms: the core DAC technology apparently works, but something in the surrounding industrial infrastructure didn't, and as of the disclosure, Occidental was still evaluating the repair timeline.

The slipping timeline, in full

Stratos was originally slated to launch by the end of 2024. That slipped to end of 2025. Now, with construction largely finished and initial commissioning complete but this new component issue unresolved, even that date is no longer firm. For a project positioned as proof that direct air capture could scale from pilot-plant size to genuinely industrial capacity, a third consecutive delay for reasons unrelated to the core technology is not a reassuring pattern, whatever the specific cause turns out to be.

Why the cost question matters more than the delay

Even once Stratos is running, its bigger test is economic, not mechanical. Direct air capture has long been benchmarked against a $100-per-ton target — the price at which removing a ton of CO2 from the air becomes cheap enough for broad voluntary and eventually regulatory adoption. Current DAC costs across the industry remain well above that, with even optimistic 2026 company targets landing closer to $100–$200 per ton rather than below it. Stratos, backed by a joint venture between Occidental's 1PointFive subsidiary and a $550 million BlackRock infrastructure investment, is exactly the kind of scaled project that's supposed to start bending that cost curve down — but only once it's actually operating.

Why oil companies are the ones building this

It's not a coincidence that an oil and gas company is behind the world's largest DAC plant. Occidental uses enhanced oil recovery techniques that involve injecting CO2 underground to extract more oil from aging wells — infrastructure and geological expertise that transfers directly to permanently storing captured CO2 instead. That overlap is why oil-adjacent companies, not pure climate-tech startups, are building DAC at the largest scale so far, which is either a pragmatic use of existing expertise or an uncomfortable irony, depending on how charitably you read it.

The honest caveat

Direct air capture at Stratos's scale has never been operated continuously anywhere in the world, so a delay from an unexpected component issue, however unglamorous, is a genuinely useful data point: even a well-capitalized, technically sound project can be held up by ordinary industrial execution problems that have nothing to do with the underlying science. That's a normal part of building first-of-a-kind infrastructure. It's also exactly the kind of friction that keeps DAC's costs stubbornly above the price point that would make it a mainstream climate tool rather than a demonstration project.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is direct air capture?

It's a technology that pulls carbon dioxide directly out of ambient air, as opposed to capturing it at an emissions source like a power plant, and then permanently stores it underground in geologic formations.

Why has Occidental's Stratos plant been delayed?

Occidental says the latest delay stems from an issue with non-process components of the facility — unrelated to the core direct air capture technology itself, which reportedly performed as expected during initial commissioning tests.

How much does direct air capture cost per ton of CO2 removed?

Current costs across the industry remain well above the widely cited $100-per-ton target that would make DAC broadly cost-competitive, with even optimistic 2026 company targets generally landing in the $100 to $200 per ton range.

Why are oil companies building direct air capture plants?

Companies like Occidental already have geological expertise and infrastructure for injecting CO2 underground, developed through enhanced oil recovery techniques used to extract more oil from aging wells — expertise that transfers directly to permanently storing captured carbon instead.
