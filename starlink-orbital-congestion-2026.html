Space Is Getting Crowded Enough That SpaceX Is Moving Starlink

Nearly 10,000 Starlink satellites are dropping to a lower orbit in 2026 — not to improve service, but because low Earth orbit is getting dangerously congested. A new ESA report explains exactly how bad it's gotten.

By the FrontierTech Desk·September 27, 2026·7 min read

Illustration: FrontierTech.news

In 2026, SpaceX began lowering the orbits of nearly all of its roughly 10,000 operational Starlink satellites — from about 342 miles (550 km) down to roughly 298 miles (480 km). It's not a service upgrade. It's a response to a low Earth orbit environment that a new European Space Agency report describes in genuinely alarming terms.

What the ESA report actually found

ESA's 2026 Space Environment Report puts a number on a problem that's mostly been discussed abstractly until now. Roughly 16,000 satellites are currently operational, out of about 18,840 still in orbit from all of history's launches, tracked among some 46,650 total man-made objects. The real danger isn't the tracked objects, though — it's the untracked ones: an estimated 1.2 million debris fragments between 1 and 10 centimeters, and roughly 140 million pieces smaller than a centimeter, any of which can disable a satellite on impact but are too small for current tracking systems to catalog individually.

The number that stands out

Starlink satellites alone performed more than 355,000 collision-avoidance maneuvers in a single year — averaging over 40 per satellite. That's not a one-time event; it's the routine, ongoing cost of operating a constellation that size in an increasingly crowded environment, and it's a workload that scales with every additional satellite SpaceX, Amazon's Kuiper, and other operators put into orbit.

Why lowering the orbit actually helps

SpaceX's VP of Starlink Engineering, Michael Nicolls, framed the move as improving space safety on multiple fronts: a lower altitude has fewer debris objects and competing constellations to navigate around in the first place, and it increases atmospheric drag enough that a satellite that fails or reaches end-of-life deorbits and burns up far faster than it would from a higher altitude — shrinking the window during which a dead satellite becomes a long-term debris hazard rather than a self-cleaning problem.

Why this matters beyond Starlink

ESA's report says the agency's Space Environment Health Index — its composite measure of orbital congestion risk — worsened by an order of magnitude in a single year during 2025. That's the kind of number that turns "orbital debris" from an academic concern about Kessler syndrome (the cascading-collision scenario where debris creates more debris faster than it can be cleaned up) into a near-term operational risk that every satellite operator, not just SpaceX, has to actively manage.

The honest caveats

SpaceX lowering its own orbit is a genuinely responsible operational choice, and ESA's report is explicit that avoidance maneuvers alone won't fix the underlying trend — the agency states plainly that active debris removal, physically deorbiting existing junk, will be required to actually halt the growth in orbital objects, not just manage around it. No such removal capability exists yet at meaningful scale. One operator's orbit adjustment is a mitigation, not a solution to a problem that gets worse with every new constellation launched into an already-crowded band of sky.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is SpaceX lowering Starlink's orbit?

To improve space safety: a lower altitude has fewer competing objects to navigate around, and stronger atmospheric drag causes failed or defunct satellites to deorbit and burn up much faster, reducing how long a dead satellite lingers as a debris hazard.

How many collision-avoidance maneuvers do satellites actually perform?

According to ESA's 2026 Space Environment Report, Starlink satellites alone performed more than 355,000 collision-avoidance maneuvers in a single year, an average of more than 40 per satellite.

What is Kessler syndrome?

It's the scenario where orbital debris density gets high enough that collisions between objects generate more debris faster than it can naturally decay or be removed, potentially cascading into a chain reaction that makes parts of low Earth orbit unusable.

Will lowering Starlink's orbit actually solve the debris problem?

No, and ESA's report says so directly — it states that active debris removal, physically deorbiting existing junk, will be required to halt the growth in orbital objects. Orbit adjustments by individual operators help, but they don't address debris that's already up there.
