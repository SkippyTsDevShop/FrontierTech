The Ultimate Guide to Reusable Rockets

Reusability turned rocket launches from a one-time expense into a repeatable asset. Here's how it actually works, who's doing it, and how far ahead SpaceX still is.

By the FrontierTech Desk·September 28, 2026·7 min read

Illustration: FrontierTech.news

For most of the history of spaceflight, a rocket was a one-use product: build it, launch it, watch the boosters fall into the ocean, build another one for the next launch. Reusability broke that model, and it's now the single biggest reason launch costs have fallen as far as they have. Here's how it actually works, and how far the companies chasing it have really gotten.

What "reusable" actually means

A reusable rocket doesn't throw away its expensive components after one flight. In practice, that almost always means recovering the first-stage booster — the largest, most expensive part of the rocket, and the part that doesn't reach orbital velocity — by guiding it back to a controlled, vertical landing on a pad or a droneship at sea, then refurbishing and reflying it. The upper stage, which does reach orbit and typically carries less structural mass relative to its cost, is usually still expended, though that's the next frontier companies are working on.

SpaceX's Falcon 9 is the reusability benchmark

SpaceX proved vertical-landing reusability could work reliably with Falcon 9, and by 2026 the fleet-leading individual booster had completed 33 flights to space — a mission history spanning crewed launches (Crew-3 and Crew-4), ISS cargo resupply, satellite deployments, and 21 Starlink missions since its first launch in June 2021. SpaceX's stated target for that booster is 40 total flights, which would surpass the Space Shuttle Discovery's record of 39 orbital missions — notable since Shuttle was the previous generation's flagship reusability program, built on a fundamentally different and far more expensive maintenance model. Turnaround between flights can run as fast as nine days for a lightly-used booster, though this record-holding booster needed about two and a half months of refurbishment before its 33rd flight as wear accumulated.

Blue Origin just reached the milestone SpaceX hit years ago

Blue Origin's New Glenn achieved its first booster reuse in April 2026, on just its third overall flight — landing successfully on the droneship Jacklyn after refurbished engines and an upgraded thermal protection system carried it through a second reentry. It's a genuine milestone for the company, but the same mission also revealed how much distance remains: the payload, a satellite from AST SpaceMobile, was deployed into an off-nominal, too-low orbit and is expected to be lost, with AST SpaceMobile recovering the cost through insurance.

SpaceX's Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, and Starship remain the only orbital-capable rocket boosters with proven, repeated reusability at commercial scale — New Glenn's first reuse is real progress, not yet parity.

Why reusability actually saves money

A rocket's first stage typically represents the majority of a launch vehicle's total manufacturing cost, so flying it dozens of times instead of once amortizes that cost across many missions rather than writing it off after a single flight. That's the mechanism behind the launch-cost collapse of the past decade: it isn't that rockets got dramatically cheaper to build, it's that each one now does the work of many. The same economics are part of why SpaceX can commit to a $100 billion Gulf Coast spaceport buildout and why reusability is central to any company's plan for high-frequency missions like satellite constellation deployment.

What full reusability would still require

Even Falcon 9's reusability is partial — the upper stage and payload fairing recovery remain more limited. SpaceX's Starship program is explicitly designed to push toward full reusability of both stages, which is also central to its role in NASA's Artemis lunar lander plans. No company has yet demonstrated fully reusable orbital-class hardware flying at the cadence and reliability Falcon 9 achieves with its partial approach, which is the real gap between "reusability works" and "reusability has been fully solved."

Frequently Asked Questions

What part of a rocket actually gets reused?

Almost always the first-stage booster, which is recovered through a controlled vertical landing and refurbished for another flight. Upper stages, which reach orbit, are typically still expended.

How many times has SpaceX reused a single Falcon 9 booster?

The fleet-leading booster reached 33 flights in 2026, with SpaceX targeting 40 total — which would surpass the Space Shuttle Discovery's 39-mission record.

Has Blue Origin caught up to SpaceX on reusability?

Not yet. New Glenn achieved its first booster reuse in April 2026, a real milestone, but SpaceX remains the only company with proven, repeated reusability at commercial scale and flight cadence.

Why does reusability actually lower launch costs?

The first stage represents the majority of a rocket's manufacturing cost. Reflying it dozens of times spreads that cost across many missions instead of discarding it after a single flight.
