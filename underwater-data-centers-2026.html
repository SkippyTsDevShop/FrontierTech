China Just Launched the World's First Commercial Underwater Data Center

Microsoft tried sinking servers into the ocean years ago and quietly shelved the idea. China just built a commercial version anyway — a 1,300-tonne data cabin on the seafloor off Hainan, cooled for free by the Pacific.

By the FrontierTech Desk·September 21, 2026·6 min read

Illustration: FrontierTech.news

Sinking a data center into the ocean sounds like the kind of idea that gets tested once, written up as a curiosity, and shelved. Microsoft actually did exactly that with Project Natick years ago — and now China has taken the same basic concept and turned it into a running commercial business off the coast of Hainan.

What's actually operating

The facility, built by Shenzhen HiCloud Data Centre Technology in Lingshui county, became operational in October 2025 and is described as the world's first commercial underwater data center. The submerged data cabin weighs roughly 1,300 tonnes — about the mass of 1,000 passenger cars — and houses server equipment on the seafloor rather than in a conventional land-based building. It sits inside Hainan's free-trade zone, part of a broader push to develop the province's "blue economy" and attract investment.

The actual engineering case

The core advantage is free, passive cooling. As the project's manager put it, seawater "can help cool down the temperature," and running a data center underwater "can reduce energy consumption needed for cooling, helping to lower operational costs" compared to an equivalent facility on land. Cooling is one of the largest ongoing operating costs and energy draws for any data center — part of why we've covered it as a real constraint on the terrestrial data center buildout more broadly — and the ocean simply offers a cheaper, more consistent heat sink than air conditioning ever can.

This already failed once — sort of

Microsoft's Project Natick tested a very similar idea years earlier: a sealed capsule of servers submerged off Scotland's Orkney Islands. The experiment actually worked technically — Microsoft reported lower failure rates for the submerged servers than equivalent land-based ones, attributed partly to the stable temperature and nitrogen atmosphere inside the sealed capsule — but the company ultimately shelved the concept rather than commercializing it, concluding it didn't fit Microsoft's broader data center strategy at the time. China's Hainan facility is a different bet: not a sealed, self-contained research capsule, but an operating commercial facility integrated into a regional economic development strategy, with a company actually selling the resulting compute capacity.

The tradeoffs nobody's pretending don't exist

Underwater placement solves cooling costs but doesn't eliminate the harder problems every data center faces: getting power to a submerged facility, running fiber connectivity to shore, and — the one that sank plenty of underwater-infrastructure ideas before this one — being able to physically access and repair hardware that's sitting on the ocean floor rather than in a building someone can walk into. Marine environmental impact, from heat discharge into surrounding water to physical seafloor disruption, is also a real, still-developing area of scrutiny for facilities like this one.

Where this fits in the bigger data center story

Between record land-based hyperscaler spending, serious proposals for data centers in orbit, and now a working commercial facility on the ocean floor, 2026 has made one thing clear: the physical constraints of land, power, and cooling have gotten serious enough that companies are willing to build compute infrastructure in genuinely unconventional places to route around them. Whether underwater data centers scale beyond this one Hainan facility depends less on whether the cooling math works — it clearly does — and more on whether the access, maintenance, and environmental questions get resolved as cheaply as the temperature problem was.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is China's underwater data center actually operating commercially?

Yes. Built by Shenzhen HiCloud Data Centre Technology in Hainan's Lingshui county, it became operational in October 2025 and is described as the world's first commercial underwater data center, selling compute capacity rather than operating as a research pilot.

How does an underwater data center stay cool?

It uses the surrounding seawater as a passive, free cooling source, which reduces the energy that would otherwise go toward air conditioning — one of the largest ongoing operating costs for any data center — and lowers overall operating costs compared to an equivalent land-based facility.

Didn't Microsoft already try this and give up on it?

Yes, with Project Natick, a sealed capsule submerged off Scotland's Orkney Islands. It technically worked well — failure rates were lower than land-based servers — but Microsoft ultimately shelved the concept rather than commercializing it. China's Hainan facility is a different, actively commercial approach to the same underlying idea.

What are the biggest challenges with underwater data centers?

Delivering power and fiber connectivity to a submerged facility, physically accessing and repairing hardware on the ocean floor rather than in a walk-in building, and understanding the marine environmental impact of heat discharge and seafloor infrastructure all remain real, unresolved questions.
