Asteroid Mining Isn't Science Fiction Anymore — Here's Who's Actually Trying

AstroForge has already launched two real missions toward asteroids. Neither has mined anything yet, but the company's approach says a lot about where this industry actually stands.

By the FrontierTech Desk·September 28, 2026·6 min read

Illustration: FrontierTech.news

Asteroid mining has been a staple of space-industry speculation for decades — a resource-scarcity solution that always seemed to be permanently ten years away. In 2026, one company has actually put hardware in deep space to start testing the idea for real, on a budget that looks nothing like a traditional aerospace program.

The company actually doing this

AstroForge is a startup pursuing platinum-group metal extraction from near-Earth asteroids, and it has taken an unusually fast, cheap approach to getting there: build small, launch often, and treat failure as data rather than catastrophe. That philosophy shows in Odin, the company's first deep-space mission — a 105-kilogram spacecraft built in under ten months for $3.5 million, a fraction of what a traditional deep-space probe costs.

What actually happened with Odin

Odin launched in February 2025 aimed at capturing high-resolution images of asteroid 2022 OB5 using multispectral cameras for surface analysis — a reconnaissance mission, not a mining attempt. The spacecraft traveled past the Moon and transmitted signals back to Earth, but the mission ran into real trouble: communication issues, ground station complications, and problems with solar panel deployment. AstroForge describes it as a partial success and, candidly, a learning mission rather than a triumph — every technical failure became data for the next spacecraft.

Odin proved AstroForge could design, build, and launch a functioning deep-space spacecraft on a startup timeline and budget — a real capability, even though the mission's science goals weren't fully met.

What comes next

AstroForge is already building DeepSpace-2, a larger 200-kilogram spacecraft scheduled to launch in 2027 that's designed to attempt an actual asteroid rendezvous rather than a flyby — a meaningfully harder maneuver that requires matching an asteroid's orbit and velocity rather than just imaging it in passing. That's still reconnaissance, not extraction; no company has landed on, sampled, or mined an asteroid on a commercial mission at any point in this cycle of renewed interest.

Why platinum-group metals are the actual target

The economic case for asteroid mining rests on platinum-group metals — platinum, palladium, rhodium, and related elements used in electronics, catalytic converters, and industrial processes — which are rare on Earth's surface but, in theory, far more concentrated in certain metallic asteroids that never underwent the geological processes that buried Earth's own metal deposits deep in its core. Whether that theoretical abundance translates into an economically viable mission, once you account for the cost of reaching an asteroid, extracting material, and returning it to a useful location, remains the multi-billion-dollar open question the entire industry is built around.

How far this actually is from a real mining operation

Even in an optimistic scenario, the sequence from where AstroForge stands now to a commercially viable mining operation runs through several more unproven steps: successfully rendezvousing with a target asteroid, characterizing its composition well enough to confirm mineable value, developing extraction technology that's never been demonstrated in microgravity at any commercial scale, and figuring out how to process or return material economically. AstroForge's approach — fast, cheap, iterative missions rather than one enormous bet — is a genuinely different strategy than earlier asteroid-mining ventures that raised large sums and produced little hardware. Whether it's faster is a real question; whether it's cheaper to get to the same eventual milestone is already looking like yes.
